# History file: Russia, March 1801

> Researched by four parallel agents on 10 Oct 2026, then adversarially fact-checked. Dates are O.S. (Julian) unless marked N.S.;
> the gap is 11 days before 1 March 1800 O.S. and 12 days after. Confidence: high / medium / low. Use low-confidence items only as rumour.

## The imperial family on 11 March 1801

On the evening of 11 (23) March 1801 Paul I's household had lived in the newly finished Mikhailovsky (St Michael's) Castle for only about 40 days, since 1 (13) February. Paul was 46. That evening he made Alexander (23) and Constantine (21) swear their oath of allegiance again, then dined with them. The guests included their wives, Grand Duchess Maria Pavlovna (15) and Kutuzov. Between about 00:45 and 02:30 on 12 (24) March, Bennigsen and the Zubovs killed Paul in his bedroom, and the official cause given was "apoplexy". Empress Maria Feodorovna (41) briefly claimed the throne ("Ich will regieren!") until Bennigsen and the guards stopped her. Platon Zubov woke Constantine. Countess Charlotte Lieven woke the small children (Anna 6, Nicholas 4, Michael 3) and took them to their mother, then to the Winter Palace. Alexander's wife Elizabeth (22) stayed with him that night. The couple had no living child: their daughter Maria (1799–1800), rumoured to be Czartoryski's, had died the previous summer. Constantine's mistreated wife Anna Feodorovna (19) was still in Russia. She left for good later in 1801. Two daughters were abroad. Elena (16) was at Ludwigslust in Mecklenburg-Schwerin with her six-month-old son. Alexandra, Palatine of Hungary, had died in Buda of childbed fever on 4 (16) March, aged 17. Her death came a week before the coup, and the news had apparently not yet reached St Petersburg. Catherine Pavlovna (12) was Paul's lively, clever daughter. The 13-year-old Prince Eugen of Württemberg, Maria Feodorovna's nephew, had been brought to court that winter. Paul reportedly told the boy's governor, Diebitsch, that he meant to adopt him as heir instead of Alexander, and there was gossip that he would marry Catherine. After the murder the boy was hurried home to Silesia. Paul's favourite Anna Lopukhina-Gagarina (23), married for show in January 1800, and the ex-captive barber Count Ivan Kutaisov (about 41) led the court faction that had pushed the Empress aside. Note on dates: before 1 March 1800 (O.S.) the calendar gap was 11 days, and after it 12 days.

## Upbringing of the grand dukes (1777–1801)

Catherine II treated her grandsons as her own teaching project. She took Alexander (born 12/23 Dec 1777) from Paul and Maria Feodorovna soon after his birth, and later Constantine (born 27 Apr/8 May 1779) as well. She wrote moral tales and a "Grandmother's ABC" for them. In 1783 she moved them from female care to a male staff under Nikolai Saltykov, and on 13 March 1784 she sent him a rescript of instructions on hardening the boys: light clothes, plain food, no idleness. Under Saltykov came Alexander Protasov, whose daily diary survives. The Swiss republican La Harpe tutored them from 1783/84 to 1795. Muravyov taught Russian letters, history and ethics, Archpriest Samborsky religion and English, Pallas natural history, Krafft physics and Masson mathematics. Both boys were inoculated against smallpox by Dimsdale in 1781.

From the early 1790s the boys moved between two courts: Catherine's at Tsarskoye Selo and Paul's Prussian-drilled camp at Gatchina. By 1796 Paul's private army was about 2,400 men, and each grand duke had a Gatchina battalion. Catherine hurried both weddings: Alexander married Louise of Baden on 28 Sep 1793, and Constantine married Juliane of Saxe-Coburg on 15 Feb 1796 (both Old Style). In October 1793 she sounded out La Harpe about passing over Paul. He pretended not to understand and left in 1795. Alexander's letters (to Kochubey, 10 May 1796, about living privately on the Rhine; to La Harpe, 27 Sep 1797, carried by Novosiltsev) show him unwilling to rule. Catherine died on 6 Nov 1796 with no succession act made public; the story of a burned testament is unproven. Paul at once made Alexander colonel of the Semyonovsky Guards and the first military governor of St Petersburg, and Constantine chief of the Izmailovsky Guards. In April 1797 Paul's Act of Succession fixed primogeniture. Calendar note: Julian ran 11 days behind Gregorian until February 1800 (O.S.) and 12 days after.

## The night of 11–12 March 1801

Paul I was killed in his bedroom in St Michael's (Mikhailovsky) Castle on the night of 11-12 March 1801 O.S. (23-24 March N.S.). Nikita Panin was the plot's ideological author but took no part that night. He had been dismissed as vice-chancellor on 15 Nov 1800 O.S. and banished in December to his estate at Dugino, in Smolensk province. Governor-General Peter von der Pahlen ran the plot itself. On 11 March the 3rd battalion of the Semyonovsky Guards, whose chef was Alexander, took over the castle guard from the Preobrazhensky battalion. It held every post except the inner guard by the room next to the bedroom. Between about 40 and 60 officers, mostly junior, gathered for supper and drink at the quarters of the Preobrazhensky commander, Major-General Talyzin, near the Winter Palace. Pahlen stayed sober and kept Bennigsen sober too. Around 23:10 to midnight he split them into two columns. Bennigsen and Platon and Nikolai Zubov led the strike group, guided across the drawbridges by Argamakov, the castle's plats-major. Pahlen and Valerian Zubov led a smaller group of about 20 men. At about 00:30, 10 to 12 officers broke in. Accounts agree on two blows: Nikolai Zubov struck Paul on the left temple with a gold snuffbox, and Paul was then strangled with a sash or scarf. Skaryatin, Yashvil and Tatarinov are named most often as the killers. Earlier that evening Paul had supper at 21:00 with 20 places laid, Kutuzov among the guests. Leaving the table, he said "Чему быть, того не миновать" ("What will be, will be"). Alexander had agreed to an abdication only if his father's life was spared. Wylie embalmed the body and certified apoplexy. Pahlen woke Alexander and told him to go and reign. Maria Feodorovna demanded the throne ("Ich will regieren!") from about 1 to 5 a.m. Troshchinsky drafted the 12 March manifesto, which promised to rule "по законам и по сердцу" ("by the laws and heart") of Catherine. Alexander's first acts followed: the recall of Orlov's Cossacks on 12 March, an amnesty on 15 March (27 N.S.), and the abolition of the Secret Expedition on 2 April (14 N.S.). ## Hooks for the Artemiy branches

Research limits first. The web-fetch proxy refused every source domain (403 on Wikipedia, prlib.ru, Britannica, archive.org and others), and the shared search budget ran out after 12 searches. Facts marked high are confirmed by search-result text. Facts marked medium or low come from standard historiography but were not re-checked this session; check them before they shape gameplay. Calendar note: the Julian–Gregorian gap was 11 days until 28 February 1800 (O.S.) and 12 days afterwards. Pre-1800 dates below use 11 days.

The legal spine is Paul's Act of Succession. It was drafted with Maria Feodorovna on 4 January 1788 and proclaimed on coronation day, 5/16 April 1797, abolishing Peter I's decree of 5/16 February 1722, which let the sovereign choose an heir. The Act fixed male-line primogeniture: Alexander and his sons, then the next brother's line, with women inheriting only once every male dynast was gone. It gave no clear procedure for renunciation. Rules on that were codified only after the Constantine crisis of 1822–1825. In March 1801 Alexander had no living child, so if he died or renounced, Constantine (21, childless) came next by law, then Nicholas (4) and Michael (3).

The rumour material is real. Catherine II is believed to have planned to pass over Paul for Alexander, and her will was reportedly destroyed (Kurakin and Bezborodko are both named, and accounts conflict). Paul's own paternity was questioned through the Saltykov passage in Catherine's memoirs. Alexander wrote that he wanted to give up the throne. Pahlen showed Alexander a written order from Paul for his arrest. Among the children, Olga died in 1795 and Alexandra died at Buda on 4/16 March 1801, a week before the murder.

The safest place to insert Artemiy is the four-year gap in Paul's children between Constantine (1779) and Alexandra (1783). For the throne to pass to him lawfully, both Alexander and Constantine must be removed on the night of 11–12 March. Everything before 11 March can stay as recorded.
